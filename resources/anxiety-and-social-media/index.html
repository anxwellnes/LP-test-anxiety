Why scrolling feels like relief
but makes it worse

You pick up the phone because the anxiety is uncomfortable. The scrolling interrupts the feeling for a while. And then you put the phone down and the anxiety is still there, often slightly worse. This is not a coincidence. It is a mechanism.

📅 August 2026 ⏱ 10 min read

Social media is, for people with anxiety, a particularly effective trap. Not because the content is necessarily distressing, though sometimes it is, but because the mechanism of social media use and the mechanism of anxiety are closely entangled. Social media provides a form of relief from anxiety that simultaneously heightens the anxiety it is relieving. Understanding this loop is the beginning of having a different relationship with both the phone and the anxiety underneath the phone use.

Most people who struggle with anxious phone use already know, at some level, that it is not helping. They notice that they feel worse after a long session of scrolling than before. They reach for the phone anyway. The gap between knowing and doing is not a gap in understanding. It is a gap that the mechanism itself creates and maintains.

· · ·

There are three distinct mechanisms connecting anxiety and social media use, and they each reinforce each other.

The first is the variable reward schedule. Social media platforms are designed around intermittent reinforcement: the unpredictable delivery of interesting, funny, or activating content in a stream where the next item might be anything. This is the same schedule that makes gambling compulsive. The unpredictability is the mechanism: the brain's dopamine system responds more strongly to uncertain rewards than to predictable ones, which means the scroll produces a sustained compulsive engagement that predictable content would not. For anxious people, whose nervous systems are already in a heightened state of alertness, this activation compounds the underlying anxiety while providing a momentary distraction from it.

The second is social comparison. The inner critic that runs in anxious people uses the curated presentations of social media as comparison material. Other people appear to be managing their lives with more ease, more success, more connection. The critic notes this and generates the conclusions the anxiety has been waiting for: you are behind, you are falling short, what you have is not enough. This process is not a deliberate choice. It is automatic. The comparison happens before the conscious mind has evaluated the accuracy of the content being compared against.

The third mechanism is avoidance. The phone is reached for when the anxiety is uncomfortable because it reliably interrupts the anxious experience. This is functionally identical to any other avoidance behaviour: it reduces anxiety in the moment while maintaining the anxiety's intensity over time. Every scroll session that provides relief from uncomfortable feelings teaches the nervous system that the phone is the solution to discomfort, which makes the urge to reach for the phone stronger the next time discomfort arrives.

The phone is not being used instead of anxiety. It is being used to manage anxiety in a way that requires more management next time. The relief is real. The cost is also real, and it compounds.

· · ·

There is also the specific content problem. Social media streams are not neutral. They contain news, which is disproportionately threat-laden because threat-laden content generates more engagement. They contain catastrophising content: posts about global disasters, personal crises, injustice, failure. They contain arguments that are unresolvable within the format. For an anxious nervous system running a continuous threat scan, this stream of material provides the threat detection system with hours of input to process.

The overthinking that follows a social media session reflects this: the mind is processing the threat-loaded content it has consumed, generating scenarios, evaluating risks, producing the anxious rumination that the social media use was meant to interrupt. The session that was supposed to provide relief has loaded the anxiety processing queue with new material.

What anxious social media use sounds like
"I pick up my phone to distract myself and then I'm more anxious than when I started."
"I know it makes me feel worse. I do it anyway. I don't know how to not."
"I look at other people's lives and feel like I'm doing everything wrong."
"I check the news to see if something terrible has happened. Then I check again in case I missed something."
"I go on to relax and somehow an hour has passed and I feel terrible."
"I deleted the apps. I reinstalled them within two days."
· · ·

That last one is important. Deleting the apps is a popular intervention and it often provides some relief in the short term. The relief is real because the stimulation and comparison material are absent. The reinstallation, which is nearly universal, reveals the limitation of the approach: the phone use was a symptom of the anxiety, and when the symptom is removed, the underlying anxiety that was driving it remains. The discomfort that the phone was managing is still there, now without the management strategy, which makes the pull toward the phone stronger than it was before. The reinstallation is not a failure of willpower. It is the anxiety finding its way back to the mechanism that was managing it.

⚠
The comparison trap runs even when you know it's happening: Social comparison on social media does not require naivety to function. People who fully understand that social media presents curated, edited highlights of other people's lives still experience the automatic downward comparison that produces inadequacy. The knowledge does not interrupt the mechanism because the mechanism operates automatically, before conscious evaluation. Understanding that the comparison is unfair does not stop the shame it produces.
📱 With high social media use
Scroll to escape anxiety, feel temporarily better
Anxiety returns, now with comparison content added
Check news to manage uncertainty, find more threat
One hour passes without noticing
Phone down. Feeling worse than before.
✓ When anxiety reduces
Discomfort is tolerable without needing to escape it
Phone use is chosen rather than compelled
Comparison does not hit as hard, baseline is steadier
Session ends when intended, not when depleted
Phone down. No change in state.

The right column is not about having more willpower. It is about having a different baseline anxiety level. When the anxiety reduces, the discomfort that was driving compulsive phone use reduces with it. The phone becomes something you use rather than something you reach for automatically when the internal state becomes uncomfortable. The relationship with social media normalises as a consequence of the anxiety normalising, not through a separate effort to use the phone less.

This is consistent with what people report after treating anxiety: phone use reduces as a side effect, without being the direct target. The anxiety that was being managed through the scroll is lower, so the scroll is less necessary. The compulsion reduces when the thing it was managing reduces. That is what becomes available through structured work on the anxiety itself.

FAQ
Common questions
Social media does not cause anxiety in the clinical sense but reliably amplifies it. The variable reward schedule, threat-loaded content, and constant social comparison all interact with the anxiety mechanism in ways that increase baseline anxiety over time. For people who already have anxiety, social media use tends to maintain and worsen the anxiety rather than providing the relief it appears to offer in the moment.
Social media provides temporary relief from anxiety through distraction and the dopamine response of variable reward content. For anxious people, this relief is particularly appealing because anxiety is uncomfortable and social media reliably interrupts the anxious experience for brief periods. The problem is that the relief is temporary, and the mechanisms of social media actively heighten anxiety over time.
Reducing social media use consistently reduces anxiety for most people who try it. However, social media use is often a symptom of the anxiety rather than its cause, meaning the urge to scroll returns strongly in its absence. Addressing the underlying anxiety through structured CBT tends to make social media use more manageable because the need to escape into the phone reduces alongside the anxiety.
Because the mechanism producing the compulsive scrolling operates below the level where knowing things makes a difference. The variable reward schedule creates compulsive engagement that understanding does not neutralise. What changes compulsive phone use is reducing the anxiety that makes distraction feel necessary.
