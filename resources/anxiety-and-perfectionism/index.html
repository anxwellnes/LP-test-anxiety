Why high standards are often
anxiety in disguise

Perfectionism is easy to misread as a virtue. You care deeply. You hold yourself to a high standard. You want things done well. None of that is wrong. The question is what happens when the standard is not met, and what the standard is actually protecting you from.

📅 August 2026 ⏱ 11 min read

There is a version of perfectionism that is simply a high standard: the satisfaction that comes from doing something well, the proportionate disappointment when it falls short, the genuine improvement over time that careful attention produces. This kind of perfectionism is compatible with finishing things, with accepting good work as good enough, with functioning without constant distress about the quality of everything you produce.

Most people who identify as perfectionists do not have that version.

Most people who identify as perfectionists have this: an impossible standard that is never quite met, a disproportionate reaction when things fall short, an inability to enjoy a finished thing for more than a few moments before attention shifts to what is still imperfect, and an exhaustion that comes not from the work itself but from the constant evaluation of the work against a standard that was never meant to be reached. That is not high standards. That is anxiety operating in the performance domain.

· · ·

The mechanism is specific. The anxiety loop works by identifying potential threats and producing avoidance of situations where those threats might materialise. In the performance domain, the threat is imperfection, and imperfection is threatening because of what the anxiety says it means: that the work is inadequate, and therefore that the person who produced it is inadequate, and therefore that judgment, rejection, or some catastrophic consequence will follow.

Perfectionism is the avoidance behaviour this produces. If the work is perfect, no one can find fault. If no one can find fault, the feared judgment cannot arrive. The impossible standard is not a standard at all. It is a protection against assessment. The perfectionism is not about the work being good. It is about the anxiety around what an imperfect outcome would mean.

This is why the standard keeps moving. Reach it, and the anxiety immediately identifies a new level of potential inadequacy. Submit the thing, and the anxiety immediately begins cataloguing what could have been better. Receive positive feedback, and the anxiety immediately generates reasons why the feedback is wrong or will not last. The standard cannot be met because it is not a standard. It is a moving target generated by a threat detection system that has decided imperfection is dangerous and is trying to maintain the illusion that perfection is possible if you just work harder.

You are not demanding too much of yourself. The anxiety is demanding something impossible, and calling it your standards.

· · ·

The relationship between perfectionism and the inner critic is direct and brutal. The inner critic is the voice that runs the evaluation, and it is not neutral. Every output is measured against the impossible standard. Every gap between the output and the standard is logged as evidence of inadequacy. The self-criticism that follows is not constructive. It does not improve future performance. It functions as a punishment for having produced something imperfect, which is to say it functions as a punishment for having produced anything at all.

People with significant anxiety-driven perfectionism often describe a pattern where the creative or productive process has become almost entirely joyless. There is anxiety before starting, anxiety during the process, anxiety about finishing, and anxiety about the finished thing. Any period of relative calm is quickly ended by the inner critic identifying the next inadequacy. The thing that was supposed to bring satisfaction, the high-quality work, never actually brings it, because the satisfaction is immediately replaced by the next round of evaluation.

What anxiety-driven perfectionism sounds like
"I can't submit it. It's not ready. It's never ready."
"I spent three times as long on it as anyone else and I still think it's not good enough."
"People tell me it's great. I know they're just being kind."
"I start things and then don't finish them because they won't be as good as I need them to be."
"When something goes well I feel relieved for about an hour. Then I start worrying about the next thing."
"I think I use the high standards as a reason not to be judged. If I never finish, they can't criticise."
· · ·

That last voice is worth pausing on. If I never finish, they can't criticise.

Perfectionism and self-sabotage are closely related, and the connection runs through the same mechanism. If the work is never finished, it cannot be assessed. If it cannot be assessed, the feared negative judgment cannot arrive. Perfectionism that produces perpetual incompletion is not a failure of motivation or discipline. It is avoidance. The work stays in progress because in-progress things are protected from final evaluation in a way that finished things are not.

This is also the mechanism behind decision anxiety: the inability to commit to a choice because any committed choice can be evaluated and found wanting. Perfectionism, procrastination, and decision paralysis are the same anxiety mechanism operating in three different contexts. The function in each case is the same: to delay or prevent the moment of assessment that the anxiety has identified as threatening.

⚠
The exhaustion of protecting yourself from your own judgment: One of the least visible costs of anxiety-driven perfectionism is the sheer energy consumed by the ongoing evaluation. The exhaustion guide describes how anxiety drains energy even when you are not doing anything physically demanding. Perfectionism adds a specific layer: the constant cognitive load of monitoring the work against an impossible standard, the self-criticism when it falls short, and the anxiety about what the falling-short means. Most perfectionists are tired not from working too hard but from the relentless evaluation running alongside the work.
· · ·

There is one more piece that is important to name: the shame layer.

Anxiety-driven perfectionism is closely connected to anxiety and shame. The perfectionism is, at its root, an attempt to prevent the shame that would follow from being seen as inadequate. The impossible standard exists because the person experiences inadequacy not as a normal feature of the human condition but as a confirmation of a feared self-assessment: I am fundamentally not enough. The work must be perfect because anything less is proof of this.

This is why positive feedback often does not land. Positive feedback says the work is good. But the perfectionism is not about the work. It is about the underlying fear of inadequacy. Feedback about the work does not address the fear. The inner critic registers the feedback, finds reasons to discount it, and returns immediately to monitoring for evidence of the feared inadequacy. The fear was never about the work in the first place.

✓
What actually changes when anxiety is addressed: People who treat anxiety through structured CBT consistently report that their perfectionism reduces alongside the anxiety, without specifically targeting the perfectionism. When the threat appraisal that made imperfection feel dangerous recalibrates, the impossible standard loses its urgency. Work can be finished. Feedback can be received. The gap between the standard and the output becomes tolerable rather than catastrophic. Most people describe this not as lowering their standards but as being able to enjoy what they produce for the first time.
· · ·

If you have read this and recognised the pattern, the question worth sitting with is not "how do I stop being a perfectionist." Perfectionism is a symptom. The question is what the anxiety underneath it is about, what it is protecting against, and whether a life organised around protecting against that particular threat is the life you would choose if the threat felt less overwhelming.

For most people reading this, the answer to that last question is no. The perfectionism has been costing more than it has been protecting. The things left unfinished, the work done but never submitted, the satisfaction that never quite arrives, the exhaustion of the constant evaluation: this has been the price of managing an anxiety that could instead be addressed.

The approach that consistently works here is not to target the perfectionism directly. Trying to lower your standards by willpower is like trying to stop your heart racing by deciding not to be anxious. What works is addressing the anxiety mechanism driving the perfectionism: the threat appraisals that made imperfection feel dangerous, the shame layer that equates the work with the worth of the person who produced it, and the avoidance cycle that has kept things in permanent incompletion.

Structured CBT with a licensed therapist targets exactly these mechanisms. The perfectionism reduces not because you decide to accept imperfection but because the anxiety that required perfection as protection has been recalibrated. Most people notice the change within the first few weeks, specifically in their relationship with finishing things and in the quieting of the inner critic's evaluation after the work is done.

If you have been managing this for years, that recalibration is available. It is what the next step looks like.

It is the anxiety mechanism applying threat appraisals to performance: the fear that an imperfect outcome will produce catastrophic consequences, typically judgment or evidence of fundamental inadequacy. The perfectionism is not about the work being good. It is about the anxiety around what an imperfect outcome would mean about the person who produced it.
Anxiety causes perfectionism through the same threat appraisal mechanism it uses elsewhere. In the performance domain, the anxiety identifies imperfection as a potential source of harm. Perfectionism is the avoidance behaviour this produces: if the work is perfect, no one can find fault, and the feared judgment cannot arrive. Like all avoidance behaviours, it provides temporary relief while maintaining and strengthening the underlying pattern over time.
The key distinction is what drives the standard and what happens when it is not met. Healthy high standards are driven by genuine care about quality and produce proportionate disappointment when not met. Anxiety-driven perfectionism is driven by fear of what imperfection means, produces intense distress when standards are not met, and sets standards calibrated not by what is achievable but by what would make the feared outcome impossible. Healthy standards can be met. Perfectionism standards never can.
Perfectionism maintains anxiety through several mechanisms: it prevents the experience of finishing things and finding they were good enough, which would challenge the anxiety's predictions. It generates chronic self-criticism through the inner critic. It produces procrastination and avoidance of tasks where imperfection is possible. And it exhausts the person, which reduces capacity to manage the anxiety and leaves less energy for everything else.
CBT for anxiety addresses the threat appraisals driving the perfectionism: the belief that imperfection will be catastrophic, and the avoidance behaviours that prevent disconfirmation of these beliefs. As the anxiety reduces, the impossible standards typically reduce with it. Most people describe this not as lowering their standards but as being able to enjoy what they produce, and to finish things, for the first time.
