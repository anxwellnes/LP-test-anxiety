Why you are tired
even when you have
done nothing

It is the end of the day. You haven't been anywhere. You haven't done anything particularly demanding. And you are completely, inexplicably drained. You don't understand why. You feel like you should be fine.

📅 July 2026 ⏱ 11 min read

You are not lazy. You are not imagining it. The exhaustion is real, and it has a cause, and the cause is not that you haven't done enough to earn tiredness. The cause is that your nervous system has been doing something extremely effortful all day, invisibly, and the energy it has consumed is exactly as real as the energy you would have used had you spent the same hours moving furniture.

Here is what has been happening while you have been "doing nothing."

· · ·

Your anxiety loop has been running. That means your sympathetic nervous system, the one responsible for the fight-or-flight response, has been in a state of elevated activation for most of the day. Not at the full intensity of an acute panic response. At the lower but sustained level of chronic anxiety: a constant background hum of readiness, vigilance, and low-grade alarm.

Maintaining that state costs energy in the same way that holding a muscle under tension costs energy. You don't have to be running for your muscles to tire. You just have to be tensed. The body in chronic anxiety is tensed, constantly, in ways that never fully release because the perceived threat never fully resolves.

Your heart has been beating slightly faster than it needs to. Your muscles, particularly in your neck, shoulders, jaw, and upper back, have been holding tension that only partially releases between moments of conscious relaxation. Your adrenal glands have been producing and clearing stress hormones on a cycle that runs whether or not anything specific is happening. Your digestive system has been suppressed. Your immune system has been working around the shifted priorities of a nervous system that has decided threat management is more urgent than maintenance.

None of this is visible. None of it is dramatic. All of it has an energy cost that compounds over hours.

The exhaustion is not about what you did today. It is about what the anxiety has been doing while you were doing nothing. It has been running the threat response. All day. On your behalf. Without a rest period.

· · ·

There is also the cognitive piece, which is harder to see but arguably more draining.

If you have significant anxiety, your brain has been running a continuous monitoring process alongside whatever else you were doing. Scanning the environment for threats. Running probability estimates on upcoming situations. Replaying yesterday's conversation for signs that something went wrong. Pre-emptively catastrophising next week. Monitoring your own internal state for signs that the anxiety is getting worse. Running the overthinking cycle on anything that has not yet been resolved.

This monitoring is not a choice. It is automatic, continuous, and consuming a proportion of your cognitive resources that is simply unavailable for other things. It is why, after a day of doing nothing physically demanding, you can feel mentally used up. The mental work that the anxiety has been doing is real work, even though it does not feel productive and does not produce anything you can point to.

· · ·

And then there is the sleep problem, which completes the cycle.

Anxiety disrupts sleep in specific ways. Difficulty falling asleep because the mind doesn't switch off at night. Waking in the early hours with worry that was apparently waiting for a moment of quiet to start running. Dreams that are anxious in tone and don't feel restorative. Lying still but not resting, because the nervous system that is supposed to use sleep for repair and consolidation is still running at elevated activation, waiting for a threat that might arrive.

The result is that you are not actually sleeping the way the body needs to sleep. You are going unconscious and returning, and some restoration happens, but the parasympathetic restoration that deep sleep is supposed to provide is being interrupted by a nervous system that will not fully stand down. You wake still carrying the previous day's deficit. Then the anxiety mechanism runs for another day on top of that deficit. Then the next night's sleep is disrupted by an even more depleted system. The cumulative exhaustion deepens steadily.

💡
Why rest doesn't fix it: This is why a weekend of doing nothing, or a holiday, or a week in bed, does not restore the energy in the way that feels like it should. The rest reduces the demand on the system, but it doesn't turn the anxiety mechanism off. The exhaustion keeps accumulating at a slower rate. You return from the rest somewhat better, then the anxiety mechanism starts running at full speed again within days, and the deficit rebuilds. Rest is necessary but insufficient. What fixes the exhaustion is fixing what is producing it.
· · ·

There is one more layer to this, which is the performance layer.

If you have significant anxiety, a proportion of your energy each day goes toward making it invisible. Appearing composed when you don't feel composed. Managing the distance between what you are experiencing and what you are showing. Keeping the anxiety from being detected. This performance has a cost that is separate from the physiological cost of the anxiety itself, and it is often the piece that is most exhausting at the end of a social day.

People describe coming home from seeing friends, or finishing a meeting, or getting through a family event, and feeling like they have run a race. Not because the event was genuinely demanding, but because of the energy consumed by simultaneously experiencing anxiety and performing the absence of it. The experience of arriving home and finally being able to stop performing is often the moment the exhaustion becomes fully visible. The energy that was being held in tension by the performance releases all at once, and what is underneath it is profound fatigue.

Most people living with anxiety have been this tired for so long that they have stopped noticing it as unusual. They have recalibrated their baseline to exhausted, the way you stop smelling something that has been in the room long enough.

· · ·

Here is what changes when the anxiety is actually addressed.

Not managed. Not coped with. Addressed at the mechanism level, through structured CBT that changes the calibration of the threat response system. When the sympathetic nervous system stops running at elevated baseline activation, the energy that was being consumed by the activation becomes available for other things. The sleep that had been disrupted by the activated nervous system begins to actually restore. The cognitive monitoring that was consuming background processing capacity reduces. The performance layer becomes thinner as the anxiety it was hiding becomes less intense.

Most people who treat anxiety through structured programmes name energy as one of the earliest and most noticeable changes. Not a dramatic transformation. A quieting. The background hum reduces. The tiredness at the end of a normal day is proportionate to the day rather than overwhelming. Waking up does not feel like starting already in deficit.

The energy was there. The anxiety was consuming it.

If you have been tired for so long that you have stopped expecting anything different, that is worth sitting with for a moment. The exhaustion you have adapted to as a baseline is not your natural state. It is a symptom. And symptoms of anxiety are among the most reliably treatable things in mental health.

The question is not whether the fatigue is treatable. It is whether this is the year you address it, or whether another year passes with the same deficit, the same performance, the same waking up already tired.

The next section describes what actually reducing the anxiety looks like, and what that does to the exhaustion that has been running alongside it.

What reducing the anxiety actually does to the tiredness

The exhaustion described in this guide is not a side effect of anxiety. It is a direct output of the anxiety mechanism running continuously without adequate rest periods. Treating the anxiety through structured CBT does not just reduce the anxiety. It reduces the metabolic and cognitive cost of the anxiety, which is what has been producing the tiredness.

People who work with a therapist on anxiety consistently report that energy improves alongside everything else, often within the first few weeks. Not because the therapist addressed the tiredness. Because when the threat response recalibrates, the body stops burning energy maintaining a state of readiness it no longer needs.

The sleep improves. The background monitoring quiets. The performance layer gets thinner. The energy that was being consumed by all three of those things becomes available. People describe it as feeling like themselves again, which is another way of saying they feel like a person who is not permanently exhausted.

That outcome is available. It requires addressing the source, not the symptom.

Common questions
Anxiety activates the sympathetic nervous system, which puts the body in a physiological state of readiness for threat. Maintaining this state requires significant metabolic energy: the heart works harder, muscles hold tension, stress hormones are continuously produced and cleared, and the brain runs heightened surveillance. All of this accumulates over hours and days, producing exhaustion that is disproportionate to any physical activity performed. The anxiety and the body guide explains each mechanism in detail.
If anxiety is present, a day of doing nothing physically is not a day of doing nothing metabolically. The threat detection system has been running the same activation processes it would run in a genuinely demanding situation. These processes have a real energy cost regardless of whether any physical action is taken. The exhaustion is real, proportionate to the metabolic cost of what the anxiety has been doing, and not a sign of laziness or weakness.
Chronic anxiety can produce persistent fatigue through several mechanisms: the ongoing metabolic cost of the sustained threat response, sleep disruption that prevents adequate restoration, the cognitive drain of continuous monitoring and worry, and the physical effects of chronic muscle tension. Addressing the anxiety directly through structured treatment consistently reduces the fatigue alongside the anxiety.
Rest doesn't fully restore energy when anxiety is present because the anxiety mechanism continues to run during rest. Sleep is disrupted by the activated nervous system. Relaxation periods are interrupted by worry. The body cannot fully enter the parasympathetic recovery state it needs because the sympathetic activation of anxiety is still running. Rest helps somewhat, but the energy deficit keeps accumulating as long as the anxiety mechanism is active.
Recovering requires addressing the anxiety mechanism producing the exhaustion, not just adding more rest. Structured CBT reduces the sustained threat response consuming the energy, which allows the nervous system to spend more time in the parasympathetic recovery state. Most people report significant improvement in energy levels as one of the earliest changes, often within the first few weeks of treatment.
